# Believable festival world generation

## The temporary city pattern

The most useful thing buried in festival planning literature is that successful festivals are laid out as temporary cities, not as piles of attractions. The UK Health and Safety Executive frames site design around whether people can arrive, enter, circulate, exit, and evacuate safely, and for outdoor sites it recommends breaking the problem into zones such as arrival and exit points, viewing areas, and the spaces around attractions like stages or bars. Texas concert safety guidance likewise expects a scaled site drawing that shows the stage, main audience area, entries and exits, perimeter fencing, vendor booths, first-aid stations, and public facilities, and it recommends giving the site grid designations so responders can locate incidents quickly. At the extreme end, Maha Kumbh 2025 planned sanitation over 4,000 hectares divided into 25 sectors. The repeated pattern is sectorization first, content placement second. citeturn13view0turn13view1turn15view0turn29view0

That logic shows up in the festivals players already recognize as feeling “real.” Glastonbury explicitly describes itself as many different festivals converging on one countryside site, with distinct named areas and some of the best experiences found on the outskirts and off the beaten track. Bonnaroo separates its campground homeland, Outeroo, from the core performance zone and distributes plazas through the campgrounds as nearby public gathering places with toilets, showers, information, medical help, art, and activities; those plazas are visible from far away via large numbered balloons and function as home-base landmarks. Electric Forest centers identity around Sherwood Forest, which organizers describe as the heart of the event, with clustered trees, hundreds of hammocks, and surprise gatherings. The design lesson is blunt: generate neighborhoods with recurring amenity bundles and landmarks, not homogeneous noise across an infinite field. citeturn21view1turn19view0turn19view1turn22search7

## The stage and crowd grammar

Stage placement is less about pretty symmetry and more about controlled separation. Manchester’s open-air concert guidance recommends spreading multiple stages or marquees throughout the site and holding simultaneous performances in different places to reduce crowd convergence and sound-system interference. It also recommends focusing loudspeakers on the audience, considering distributed delay towers, and using site design and stage orientation early to minimize disturbance. HSE adds that poor site design can create dangerous crossflow, and that stalls or concessions can obstruct movement near entrances and exits during busy periods. For worldgen, that means the main stage wants a large front bowl and clean approaches, while side stages want enough removal to feel like separate destinations rather than annexes leaking into each other. citeturn31view0turn23view0turn13view1turn14view0

Real festival layouts are also shaped by three movement phases: arrival, on-site circulation, and departure. HSE recommends designing all three deliberately, including phase smoothing with good catering and welfare facilities before and after the main event. That is a strong clue for game feel. Food, shaded rest, and minor activities are not just filler around the edges. They are flow regulators that absorb pressure when too many people are trying to do the same thing at once. A map that has only attractions and roads will feel wrong because real festivals use intermediate spaces to soften surges. citeturn14view0turn13view1

## The amenity and vendor grammar

Amenities are what make a festival map feel legitimate, because real organizers plan them from the beginning rather than shoving them into leftover gaps. Oneida County’s temporary-event guidance requires toilets in accessible, convenient locations, handwashing near toilets and food-preparation areas, and notes that food-service areas require toilets and handwashing while craft-vendor areas may not. It also says large numbers of urinals and toilets should be placed near stages and that pedestrian access to sanitation must not impede emergency or servicing vehicles. The Purple Guide Lite similarly bakes sanitation ratios into early planning, recommending more female toilets than male toilets, plus accessible units and hand hygiene. Bonnaroo distributes hydration and cooling stations around the venue, and Glastonbury offers free water refills at every tap and many WaterAid bars, alongside five information points that hand out maps and other essentials. In game terms, every district needs a welfare bundle, not just scenery. citeturn12view0turn34view0turn19view2turn33view0

Vendor geometry is stricter than it looks from ground level. Sonoma County’s festival-layout diagram shows vendor booths arranged in long linear rows with 20-foot aisle or emergency-access corridors and maximum run lengths, while cooking booths are limited to single rows with shorter maximum runs. Sacramento and Arvada both require hot food vendors to appear on the site plan and to maintain clearances from structures, tents, canopies, or rides. This strongly supports your instinct that merchandise vendors belong on market streets that straddle roads, but food wants its own more deliberate courts or pockets where queues, seating, servicing, and smoke can exist without choking the main browse corridor. citeturn25view0turn25view1turn25view2turn34view0

There is another useful operational split here: front-of-house abundance, back-of-house concentration. Glastonbury says it has over 400 food traders spread across the site, but it also maintains an on-site wholesale market to reduce delivery vehicles, and it stores 2 million litres of water with 7 km of private water mains. That is a real-world example of festival feel being produced by many distributed food and drink touchpoints that are secretly supported by a much simpler logistics backbone. For procedural generation, the player-facing map can look wildly varied while the hidden supply graph stays disciplined. citeturn33view0

## The woods, water, and refuge grammar

Nature is not neutral dressing. It changes how the festival reads. Electric Forest describes Sherwood Forest as a central social world, with hammocks in clustered trees, gathering spaces, art, and secret parties, while Glastonbury openly tells people that many of its best experiences are on the outskirts and off the beaten track. HSE, however, warns that poorly maintained or badly lit pedestrian routes become hazards, and that obstructive stalls or concessions can create congestion. The synthesis is clear enough for worldgen: drum circles, hammocks, strange art pockets, and other “found” moments belong just off primary routes, close enough to main desire lines to be discoverable, far enough to feel discovered. Deep wilderness with no relationship to the main circulation graph reads less like a real festival and more like random biome scatter. citeturn22search7turn21view1turn13view1

Water and trees are especially easy to fake badly. Manchester’s guidance states that water carries sound well across lakes and rivers, topography affects the direction and distance sound travels, and trees do not provide an effective noise barrier. It also says loudspeakers should face away from noise-sensitive premises. That matters directly to your lakes, woods, and stage logic. A lake can create a great scenic edge, but it can also throw bass into the quiet zone on the opposite shore. A patch of trees can create intimacy and visual concealment, but unless landform or built elements are doing the real work, that forest is not an acoustic wall. If you want believable quiet refuges, topography and intentional stage orientation matter more than “more trees.” citeturn23view0turn24view1turn31view0

Support landmarks matter almost as much as attractions. Bonnaroo’s plazas are visible from far away and function as home bases. Glastonbury’s information points provide maps, emergency contact help, sunscreen, ear plugs, and other practical support. Coachella distributes three accessibility service hubs around the grounds, warns that the farthest points can require a three-quarter-mile traverse in direct sunlight, and keeps accessible cart service on the periphery rather than through the venue bowl for public-safety reasons. Players tolerate scale when the map keeps giving them legible return-home markers and service anchors. They do not tolerate scale when every node has the same semantic weight. citeturn19view0turn33view0turn20view1

## Spec-ready worldgen changes

If this were being turned into OpenSpec changes, these are the highest-leverage changes to make first.

- **Generate sectors before props.** Classify land into stage core, campground or plaza, market street, food court, refuge woods, waterfront, and utility-shadow sectors before any asset placement. That matches HSE’s zone-based crowd design, Kumbh’s sector logic, Bonnaroo’s plaza system, and Glastonbury’s named-area model. citeturn13view1turn29view0turn19view0turn21view1

- **Make stages edge-oriented anchors.** Put the main stage on the edge of a large pedestrian bowl facing inward, then place secondary stages far enough apart to reduce simultaneous crowd pull and sound bleed. Run an acoustic validation pass that considers speaker direction, topography, lakes, and whether a quiet zone sits across water from a loud zone. citeturn31view0turn24view1turn23view0

- **Treat roads as classes, not one graph.** Separate promenade paths, market streets, service or fire lanes, and cart-only periphery routes. HSE treats pedestrian and vehicle mixing as a real crowd hazard; Oneida calls for separate access roads where needed; Coachella keeps cart movement on the periphery for safety. citeturn13view1turn12view0turn20view1

- **Populate vendor rows by corridor rules.** Merchandise vendors can occupy long double rows along roads or promenades if you preserve cross-aisles and emergency access. Cooking and food vendors should break into courts or shorter runs with setbacks, servicing room, and less through-traffic conflict. citeturn25view0turn25view1turn25view2

- **Attach welfare bundles to hubs.** Plazas, stage flanks, food courts, and major crossroads should automatically spawn toilets, water, handwashing or cleaning equivalents, info or help, shade or cooling, and sometimes medical presence. Increase density near stages and food zones; reduce it in quieter craft stretches. That is how the official guidance differentiates food areas, craft areas, and stage areas. citeturn12view0turn34view0turn19view2turn33view0

- **Place secret content at gradient edges.** Woods, hill shoulders, lake edges, and the backsides of districts should spawn lower-intensity social pockets such as drum circles, hammocks, odd art, and bubble-top-off kiosks. That is an inference from how Electric Forest and Glastonbury locate memorable off-beat experiences, combined with HSE’s warning not to obstruct primary movement corridors. Bubble refill booths should behave like specialized plaza amenities, not random isolated props. citeturn22search7turn21view1turn13view1turn19view0

- **Give every district a return-home landmark and stable ID.** Bonnaroo uses big numbered balloons; Texas guidance recommends grid designations; Glastonbury runs fixed info points; Eventeny and OnePlan both rely on layered mapped objects and clear labels. Your generator should output persistent `sector_id`, `landmark_id`, and `service_lane_id` values so scheduling, NPC behavior, debugging, and map rendering all talk about the same place. citeturn19view0turn15view0turn33view0turn16view0turn16view1

## Planning tools and source stack

OnePlan is the clearest commercial model for outdoor event-planning logic. It is map-first, to-scale, explicitly supports barriers, tents, toilets, traffic controls, signage, parking, staff placement, and scenario planning, and it markets itself directly for festivals and other outdoor events. If your project needs an internal editing model, this points toward layered site objects on an accurate map plane rather than a loose bag of spawned set dressing. citeturn16view0

Eventeny is more useful for vendor and attendee map semantics. It supports drag-and-drop booth creation, exact scaling, zoning and layering, public and private views, searchable maps for attendees, clickable booth details, role-based sharing, printing, and live updates. That is production proof that vendor layout works best as a structured layer on top of the site graph, not as an afterthought squeezed into whatever gaps remain. citeturn16view1turn16view2

The highest-value document stack to feed an agent is: HSE venue and crowd docs for zone logic and failure modes; Manchester’s acoustic guidance for stage separation, speaker direction, and the bad news about lakes and trees; Oneida plus Purple Guide Lite for sanitation and welfare distribution; Sonoma, Sacramento, and Arvada for vendor-row and fire-lane geometry; Bonnaroo, Glastonbury, Electric Forest, and Coachella for real festival district patterns, amenity landmarks, and scale; OnePlan and Eventeny for the data structures actual planners use. The common message across all of them is simple: believable festivals are composed from districts, corridors, service layers, and landmarks.
