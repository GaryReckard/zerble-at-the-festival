# Local Encodable Layout Rules for an Infinite Procedural Festival

## What the sources imply

The strongest pattern in the sources is not “scatter more assets.” It is “make legible districts.” Places read as designed when paths, edges, nodes, and landmarks work together; when arrival is staged; when rare services live at obvious hubs; and when adjacent areas are mediated by seams, buffers, or shared courts instead of two unrelated frontages crashing into each other. Real festivals do this explicitly. Bonnaroo organizes camping around strategically located plazas that bundle toilets, showers, info, medical, art, and activities, and marks them with large numbered balloons visible from far away. Glastonbury splits the site into named areas with distinct atmospheres, including Woodsies, Green Fields, the Healing Field, the Park, and Strummerville, instead of treating every acre as interchangeable event surface. Kevin Lynch’s city-image framework describes the same structure in abstract terms: districts, edges, nodes, landmarks, and paths. citeturn13view0turn14search0turn14search1turn16search4turn31view0

For your architecture, that matters because nearly all of the useful “feel” signals are expressible as bounded local checks. The high-value moves are local: put gateways at transitions, keep view corridors on approach axes, treat cross-hub borders as seams, formalize desire lines, cluster amenities at recognizable nodes, and make quiet in-between zones behave like low-intensity festival neighborhoods instead of leftover noise. The sourced numbers are strongest for market geometry, walkable clearances, comfort-station spacing, and connector-path widths. Where no hard number exists in the literature, I mark the rule as a **derived heuristic** rather than pretending it is a code requirement. citeturn19view0turn19view1turn23view0turn25view0turn26view0turn37view0

## Cross-hub borders

The design move for neighboring hubs is not total separation. It is **border typing**. Real festivals repeatedly use named intermediate zones, shared support nodes, and landscape changes to mediate adjacency. Bonnaroo’s camping areas are not endless raw campsites; they are organized around plazas that act as public anchors with toilets, showers, info, medical, art, and activities, and the plaza balloon itself is a landmark. Glastonbury gives large chunks of land over to atmosphere districts such as Woodsies, the Green Fields, the Healing Field, Strummerville, and the Park. Lynch’s language fits cleanly here: edges can be barriers, but they can also be seams that join related regions. citeturn13view0turn14search0turn14search1turn16search4turn31view0

For your specific **cross-hub vendor-row** problem, the local convention to encode is this: **two fronts may meet only if the space between them has been promoted to a real street**. If the local window shows two vendor rows heading toward each other across a road or shared border, branch deterministically into one of three outcomes. First, **shared market street**: keep one continuous frontage logic on both sides and preserve the sourced 10–12-foot browse aisle, with no dead-end circulation. Second, **shared amenity court**: if both hubs want food or support in the same place, merge them into a single court or plaza instead of duplicating. Third, **soft edge**: if the adjacency is stage-to-market, camp-to-market, or quiet-to-loud, convert the border band into trees, hammocks, picnic shade, potties, water, or an info/welfare node. This is exactly how a place stops reading “two algorithms overlapped” and starts reading “two neighborhoods meet here.” citeturn19view0turn19view1turn13view0turn31view0turn34view0

The other key convention is **orientation-away**. Front doors, menus, lights, and arches should point inward toward each hub’s own core or along a shared spine, not outward into the neighboring district unless you have intentionally designated that edge as a market street. UIowa’s gateway guidance treats entries as formal transitions tied to circulation; its buffer-edge guidance treats soft treatment and screening as the proper answer for ambiguous perimeters. That is the right local approximation for your hub borders. citeturn34view0

## Transition zones and sparse amenities

Your “dead air” should stop behaving like leftover land and start behaving like a **low-intensity festival district**. The sources converge on that. NPS campground design treats campgrounds as neighborhoods with easy access to services, central group facilities, connector trails, screening, and simple circulation. Glastonbury distinguishes lively and quiet camping zones, wooded zones, healing zones, and campfire-led areas. Bonnaroo guarantees a plaza nearby in camping. The throughline is obvious: the in-between is still programmed, just at lower energy. citeturn37view0turn37view2turn32view0turn13view0

A useful local recipe for transition terrain is: one **formal 6–8 ft path**, one **small landmark**, one **amenity bundle or social triad**, and one **landscape room** such as a hammock grove, camp cluster, lakeside picnic patch, or wooded clearing. Project for Public Spaces calls this triangulation: seating becomes far more socially effective when it faces something worth watching and something else useful is nearby. That gives you a clean local test for anti-empty-space worldgen. A hammock by itself is filler. A hammock grove facing a lake edge with a nearby snack booth or refill stand becomes a place. citeturn37view0turn29search1turn29search4

For **bubble vendors**, the clean analogy is not a random merchant. It is a **rare utility node**. Real festivals keep rare-but-essential supports sparse, visible, and map-readable: Bonnaroo plazas are explicit home-base anchors; Glastonbury’s information points, water bars, and property lock-ups are limited in number and marked on the map. So bubble vendors should attach to obvious nodes: a gateway before a stage, a crossroads where two collector roads meet, a lake crossing, a shared seam court between hubs, or a camp plaza. That makes them feel placed, and it also quietly teaches the player how the festival works. citeturn13view0turn32view0turn33view2

For **drum circles**, the best pattern in the sources is not a quantitative one. It is typological. Woodsies’ Tree Stage, Strummerville’s campfire scene, and Shangri-La’s installation forest all place communal or intimate music/social activity inside wooded or semi-wooded landscape rooms rather than on raw arterial frontage. Pair that with NPS connector-path guidance and you get a clean local rule: a drum circle needs a real clearing, a formal path in, and tree-density around it strong enough that it feels found rather than dropped. citeturn14search4turn16search4turn16search5turn37view0

## High-ROI linter checks to add first

If you want the biggest perceptual gain per engineering hour, the first lints to add are these. **`gateway_precedes_stage`**: reject arches that are lateral to or behind the main stage instead of on the approach. **`border_frontage_conflict`**: reject any border where two unrelated active fronts meet without a typed seam. **`market_row_continuity`**: reject vendor rows with dead-end browse aisles or arbitrary gaps. **`amenity_bundle_score`**: require high-intensity nodes to co-locate at least three support functions. **`transition_path_present`**: require inter-hub terrain above some traffic potential to contain at least one formal 6–8-foot connector path. **`drum_circle_clearing_score`**: require a low-center/high-ring tree pattern plus one formal path for drum circles. Those six checks hit the exact failure modes your playtests are reporting: collision, emptiness, weak legibility, and “props, not places.” citeturn34view0turn19view0turn23view0turn37view0turn31view0

## Open questions and limits

The weakest sourced areas are the ones you would expect: there are good hard numbers for stalls, aisles, pedestrian widths, toilets, showers, and connector trails, but not for the “right” **stage-approach setback**, **dramatic reveal distance**, **bubble-vendor spacing**, or **drum-circle clearing diameter** as matters of feel. Where the table gives a number in those areas, I marked it as a **derived heuristic**, not a code value.

The practical consequence is straightforward. Your most defensible lints are the market-geometry, path-width, amenity-bundle, comfort-station, hierarchy, and border-seam rules. The more atmospheric rules should be implemented as scored heuristics and tuned by playtest rather than treated as absolutes.
